Rethinking Self-Attention Parameterization: Decoupling Self- and Cross-Token Interactions

Although mathematically sound, the standard parameterization of Transformer self-attention based on \(W_QW_K^\top\) has two overlooked structural limitations that may hinder optimization. First, the skew-symmetric component has no effect on self-interactions, introducing unnecessary parameter redundancy. Second, the symmetric component is jointly responsible for modeling both self- and cross-token interactions, potentially inducing implicit multi-objective gradient conflicts. To overcome these limitations, we propose a decoupled parameterization that employs independent parameter sets for self- and cross-token interactions. This design eliminates both the parameter redundancy and the gradient interference, potentially improving optimization efficiency.

1. Starting Point: Two Types of Token Interactions

The Query-Key interaction is the central mechanism of self-attention. Given an input sequence \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\), a standard Transformer first projects it into queries and keys

\[ Q=XW_Q,\quad K=XW_K, \]

where \(W_Q,W_K\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) are the projection matrices for query and key, respectively. The attention score matrix is then computed as

\[ S=QK^\top=XW_QW_K^\top X^\top. \]

The expression \(x_iW_QW_K^\top x_j^\top\) is a classic parameterization of pairwise interactions, and one rarely questions whether it is equally suitable for every entry of \(S\).

A closer examination, however, reveals an important distinction. The off-diagonal entries of \(S\) capture interactions between distinct tokens, whereas the diagonal entries measure the interaction of each token with itself. Although both are computed using the same unified formula, their underlying mathematical structures are fundamentally different.

For interactions between distinct tokens (\(i\neq j\)), the score is a general bilinear form

\[ s_{ij}=x_iW_QW_K^\top x_j^\top, \]

which is in general asymmetric — the score from token \(i\) to token \(j\) is typically not equal to the score in the reverse direction, i.e.,

\[ x_iW_QW_K^\top x_j^\top \;\ne\; x_jW_QW_K^\top x_i^\top. \]

In fact, prior studies have shown that such asymmetric or directional attention pattern naturally emerges in autoregressive Transformers and plays an important role in their information processing[1]. Mechanistic circuit analysis has also clarified this point, showing that the query and key subspaces serve distinct, asymmetric roles in in-context learning[2],[3].

However, when the query and key correspond to the same token (\(i=j\)), the score becomes a quadratic form \[ s_{ii}=x_iW_QW_K^\top x_i^\top, \] where the same input token \(x_i\) appears on both sides. Intuitively, there should be no asymmetry in a token's interaction with itself.

2. Self-Interaction Scores Depend Only on the Symmetric Component

To better understand the distinction between self-interactions and interactions between distinct tokens, we decompose \(W_QW_K^\top\) into its symmetric and skew-symmetric components. For notational convenience, let

\[ C = W_QW_K^\top. \]

We decompose \(C\) into its symmetric and skew-symmetric components as

\[ C = C_{\mathrm{sym}} + C_{\mathrm{skew}}, \]

where

\[ C_{\mathrm{sym}} = \frac{C+C^\top}{2}, \quad C_{\mathrm{skew}} = \frac{C-C^\top}{2}. \]

By construction, \(C_{\mathrm{sym}}\) is symmetric, satisfying \(C_{\mathrm{sym}}^\top=C_{\mathrm{sym}}\), while \(C_{\mathrm{skew}}\) is skew-symmetric, satisfying \(C_{\mathrm{skew}}^\top=-C_{\mathrm{skew}}\).

A particularly interesting property emerges when the query and key come from the same token. For any vector \(x\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}\), the quadratic form associated with a skew-symmetric matrix is always zero

\[ xC_{\mathrm{skew}}x^\top = 0. \]

To see this, note that the scalar \(xC_{\mathrm{skew}}x^\top\) is equal to its transpose

\[ xC_{\mathrm{skew}}x^\top = \left(xC_{\mathrm{skew}}x^\top\right)^\top = xC_{\mathrm{skew}}^\top x^\top = -xC_{\mathrm{skew}}x^\top. \]

Therefore, it must be zero. Consequently, the self-interaction score can be written entirely in terms of the symmetric component

\[ s_{ii} =x_iCx_i^\top=x_i(C_{\mathrm{sym}}+C_{\mathrm{skew}})x_i^\top=x_iC_{\mathrm{sym}}x_i^\top. \]

This reveals a subtle, yet largely overlooked, difference in the parameterization of the two types of interactions.

Self-interactions and interactions between distinct tokens have different parameterization requirements: the skew-symmetric component of \(W_QW_K^\top\) is essential for representing directional interactions between distinct tokens, but is functionally redundant for self-interactions.

3. Optimization Friction: Redundancy and Implicit Gradient Conflicts

The analysis above reveals that standard attention parameterization is not as natural or flawless as traditionally assumed. Mathematically, the parameterization is very sound: while it contains redundant parameters, their contribution is identically zero. However, when viewed through the lens of optimization, this setup exposes deeper structural friction. Specifically, while \(W_Q W_K^\top\) unifies both self- and cross-token interactions under a single formulation, this shared parameterization introduces optimization inefficiencies during gradient-based learning.

  • Parameter Redundancy in Self-Interactions. As established, the skew-symmetric component \(C_{\mathrm{skew}}\) is completely inert on the diagonal (\(xC_{\mathrm{skew}}x^\top = 0\)). This creates parameter redundancy where a subset of the model's capacity contributes zero expressive value to self-interactions, yet still remains active in cross-token interactions.
  • Implicit Multi-Objective Gradient Conflict. The symmetric component \(C_{\mathrm{sym}}\) is forced to simultaneously accommodate two distinct optimization objectives: modeling self-interactions (\(s_{ii}\)) and capturing directional cross-token relations (\(s_{ij}\)). When these two objectives demand opposing update directions, they create implicit multi-objective gradient conflicts. This shared parameterization leads to gradient interference and therefore impairs optimization efficiency.

These issues demonstrate that the current attention parameterization—though seemingly unproblematic—creates structural friction during training. This motivates us to explore an alternative attention parameterization that better accommodates the distinct requirements of the two types of interactions.

4. A Decoupled Parameterization Strategy

Motivated by the preceding observation and analysis, we propose a simple modification to the attention score parameterization. The key idea is to assign different parameterizations to the two types of interactions:

  • Inter-token interactions: retain the standard query–key bilinear form.
  • Self-interactions: replace the standard quadratic form with a new independent parameterization.

Specifically, we introduce an additional learnable symmetric matrix \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) and parameterize the self-interaction score as

\[ s_{ii}=x_i A\,\! x_i^\top. \]

The complete parameterization is now given as

\[ s_{ij}= \begin{cases} x_iW_QW_K^\top x_j^\top, & i\neq j,\\[6pt] x_iA\,\!x_i^\top, & i=j. \end{cases} \]

To reduce the number of parameters, we can further parameterize the symmetric matrix \(A\) using a low-rank factorization

\[ A=UU^\top, \]

where \(U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\) and \(r\ll d\). This parameterization guarantees that \(A\) is symmetric positive semidefinite (PSD), while reducing the number of learnable parameters from \(d^2\) to \(dr\). With this factorization, the self-interaction score can be computed very efficiently as

\[ s_{ii}=x_iUU^\top\! x_i^\top =\|x_iU\|_2^2, \]

which avoids the explicit matrix multiplication involved in computing \(x_iA\,\!x_i^\top\).

An even simpler alternative is to directly parameterize the self-interaction using a learnable vector \(v\in\mathbb{R}^d\)

\[ s_{ii}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}v_k\,x_{ik}^2, \]

which is equivalently written as

\[ s_{ii}=x_i\operatorname{diag}(v)\,x_i^\top. \]

These formulations fully disentangle the self-interaction score from \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\). Although this decoupling introduces additional learnable parameters, it eliminates the redundant parameterization and gradient conflicts discussed in previous sections, as the two families of scores now receive gradients through entirely separate parameters.
